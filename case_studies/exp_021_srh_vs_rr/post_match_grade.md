### What the memo got wrong

- **The +8pp compound lean from four same-direction adjustments had no discount for correlated overweighting — the primary calibration weakness.** Four individually justified adjustments all favored SRH: +3pp quality, +3pp toss, +1pp bowling, +1pp batting depth = +8pp total. Each was defensible on its own merits. But when all adjustments lean the same direction, the probability that ALL are correctly calibrated is lower than the probability for any individual one. Small systematic biases in the analytical frame can compound: resolving the quality range at +3pp (not +2pp), the toss range at +3pp (not +2pp), the bowling at +1pp (not +0.5pp), and the batting depth at +1pp (not +0.5pp) each favor SRH by the smallest defensible increment, yet the sum (+8pp) represents a strong directional lean. The market's 55.5% SRH (+5.5pp from 50/50) implicitly applies a compound discount that the pipeline lacks. At +6pp (with a ~25% compound discount), the prediction would have been 56% SRH (44% RR) — Brier 0.3136, still poor but 0.0228 better than 0.3364.

- **The +3pp toss advantage was built on a 4-match sample whose 75% chase win rate has an enormous confidence interval.** The Skeptic explicitly calculated: "95% CI for 75% in 4 trials: 33-95%." Despite this, +3pp was maintained based on directional consistency (all 4 toss winners chose to field, Finch's commentary, structural target-visibility argument). The reasoning was analytically defensible but epistemically fragile: the 75% statistic did more work than its sample size warranted. RR won batting first — reducing the 2026 Mullanpur chase win rate from 75% (3/4) to 60% (3/5). The structural chase advantage was likely real but closer to +2pp than +3pp. At +2pp (instead of +3pp), the base rate would have been 57% SRH — Brier 0.3249, marginally better.

- **SRH's bowling weakness was dismissed as "editorial opinion" when it had analytical substance.** The Skeptic's Challenge 4 correctly identified "inexperienced bowling under pressure" as an editorial claim from Sportstar. But the underlying signal was real: SRH's bowling XI included Malinga, Sakib Hussain, Shivang Kumar, and Praful Hinge (economy 11.59) — none with playoff experience. RR's bowling had Archer (21 wickets, proven international match-winner), Jadeja (15+ years of international experience, fresh from injury rest), and Burger (experienced international). The "pressure" framing is non-evidence, but the pedigree gap in high-stakes match experience is real and was not captured in the +1pp bowling assessment, which focused on economy rates rather than ceiling-under-pressure. The pipeline has no Entry for playoff/high-stakes bowling pedigree as distinct from regular-season bowling quality.

- **Sooryavanshi's individual impact was classified as a band-width factor (Entry 18) rather than receiving a partial midpoint adjustment for batting-first conditions.** The Skeptic's Challenge 3 classified Sooryavanshi as "a band-width factor, not a midpoint factor." But this classification interacted with the toss assessment: the +3pp toss advantage for SRH penalized RR for batting first. On a flat 214-average pitch, the combination of "batting first = disadvantage" and "Sooryavanshi = band-width only" systematically underweighted the scenario where Sooryavanshi posts 220+ batting first, compressing the structural chase advantage. Entry 27's asymmetric-uncertainty framework applies: Sooryavanshi's ceiling is directionally asymmetric when batting first — if he fires, RR's first-innings total enters 220+ territory where the chase advantage is neutralized; if he fails, the chase advantage is amplified. An asymmetric midpoint shift per Entry 27 would have reduced the effective toss penalty from +3pp to +2pp.

- **The band width of ±5pp was justified by evidence quality but the midpoint was the problem.** The ±5pp standard width produced a band of 53-63% SRH (37-47% RR). This excluded RR winning as anything above a 47% probability team — the second band miss in 21 experiments. The band miss occurred not because the width was wrong but because the midpoint was overconfident. At 56% SRH (with a compound-lean discount), the band would have been 51-61% SRH (39-49% RR) — still a miss. Only at ≤55% SRH would the band have captured the outcome with ±5pp width. Band width cannot compensate for a miscalibrated midpoint.

### What was missed

- **No mechanism exists to check whether the analytical frame is producing a systematic same-direction lean.** The Skeptic challenged individual adjustments (five separate challenges) but did not step back to ask: "All my accepted adjustments favor SRH. Is this because SRH is genuinely favored in every dimension, or because the analytical frame resolves ambiguity in one direction?" When 4+ adjustments favor the same team, a compound-lean check should ask: (a) is there a plausible RR-favorable factor not captured in any adjustment? (b) is the magnitude of the sum (+8pp) consistent with the market's assessment (+5.5pp)? (c) would the prediction change materially if 2 of the 4 adjustments were set 1pp lower? In this case, reducing toss and quality by 1pp each gives +6pp = 56% SRH — the directional call survives. The lean was directionally robust; the magnitude was the vulnerability.

- **The interaction between SRH's negative and RR's positive Mullanpur experience was noted in the contrarian case but excluded from the base rate.** SRH scored 219 at Mullanpur and lost by 6 wickets to PBKS. RR chased 222 at Mullanpur and won by 6 wickets against PBKS. Both matches against the same opponent — a natural comparison. The memo correctly included these facts in the contrarian case (Skeptic items 3-4) but did not incorporate them as a formal adjustment. A modest +0.5-1pp RR venue-comfort adjustment would have been defensible and would have partially offset the compound SRH lean. The analytical frame (all adjustments start from "SRH advantage or neutral") may have created a systematic blind spot for RR-favorable factors that didn't fit existing adjustment categories.

- **The pipeline has no explicit base-rate cap for well-matched playoff teams at neutral venues.** Both teams qualified for playoffs (SRH 1st, RR 3rd/4th). The match was at a neutral venue. Playoff teams are, by definition, the league's most competitive — the quality spread is narrower than in league matches. A neutral-venue playoff prior of 50±3pp (47-53%) would be defensible, requiring especially strong evidence for any lean beyond this range. The +8pp lean exceeded this by 5pp. The market's +5.5pp lean also exceeded it but by less. This is a structural gap: the pipeline treats playoff neutral-venue matches the same as league home-ground matches in its adjustment framework.

- **Pipeline-market convergence masked a shared analytical gap.** Both pipeline (58% SRH) and market (55.5% SRH) agreed directionally. Entry 22 checked for independence but not for shared gaps. When two independent assessments agree AND both are wrong, the error is likely in a factor neither captured. In this case, both may have underweighted the scenario where batting first + Sooryavanshi's ceiling = chase advantage neutralized, and both may have overweighted the toss advantage from a 4-match sample.

### Decisive Factor

Without detailed match scoring data (only the Polymarket resolution is recorded), the specific match-deciding moments cannot be identified. The structural factors:

1. **The most likely match scenario: Sooryavanshi and/or Jaiswal posted a high first-innings total that compressed the structural chase advantage.** The memo identified this as the "Main Uncertainty": "Whether Sooryavanshi fires in the first innings." On a flat 214-average pitch, Sooryavanshi (583 runs, SR 232, 430 PowerPlay runs) is capable of single-handedly posting a 220+ target. The memo's "What Would Change This View" section explicitly anticipated: "If Sooryavanshi and Jaiswal both fire in the PowerPlay (40+ runs in first 6 overs at SR 200+), RR's probability rises to ~45-48%." This scenario may have materialized. RR won batting first on a venue where the batting-first team had lost 3 of 4 prior 2026 matches — suggesting RR posted a total high enough to overcome the structural chase advantage.

2. **Archer's PowerPlay threat against SRH's openers may have disrupted the chase.** The memo's second scenario: "If Archer removes Head and/or Abhishek in the PowerPlay, SRH's chase becomes dependent on Ishan and Klaasen from 2-2 or worse." Archer (21 wickets, dismissed Head 3 times in T20s) was identified as the key RR bowling weapon in the chase. If Archer delivered early breakthroughs, SRH's chase on a flat pitch would have required the middle order to anchor while maintaining the required rate against a 220+ target.

3. **SRH's inexperienced bowling lineup may have been unable to contain RR's batting.** On a 214-average pitch, Malinga, Sakib, Shivang, and Hinge — none with playoff experience — may have struggled against a confident Sooryavanshi-Jaiswal opening pair. Cummins' captaincy and bowling ceiling (Entry 23) may not have been sufficient to compensate for the support bowlers' inexperience in a knockout match.

4. **The 75% chase win rate at Mullanpur was from 4 matches — the smallest sample used for a structural venue claim in the series.** RR won batting first, reducing the rate to 60% (3/5). The structural chase advantage was real but the magnitude was uncertain. The result suggests the true chase advantage at Mullanpur may be closer to +2pp than the +3pp assigned.

5. **Pipeline-market convergence reflected a shared blind spot.** Both pipeline (58% SRH) and market (55.5% SRH) favored SRH. When two independent assessments agree and both are wrong, the error is in a factor neither captured. The shared gap: neither the pipeline nor the market fully priced the scenario where batting first + Sooryavanshi's ceiling = chase advantage neutralized. The pipeline correctly identified this scenario in the contrarian case but didn't transmit it to the midpoint; the market may have similarly identified but underweighted it.
